      Fair questions

      Isn't someone already doing this?

      Hasn't NASA already searched this data?

      Partly. NASA and MIT run automatic searches on TESS data, and volunteers look too. But those searches use fixed cut-offs, mostly look at one month of data at a time, and humans only have time to vet so many faint stars. Real, shallow signals slip through. My job is to find the ones that did.

      How do you know a find is actually new?

      Every signal I detect is compared against every public TESS list: NASA's official candidates (TOIs), candidates found by independent teams and volunteers (CTOIs), all confirmed planets, and the catalog of known eclipsing binaries. I match by star ID and by position on the sky, so a known star's light leaking into a neighbour doesn't fool me. Anything that survives isn't on any of those lists.

      Do you ever check the same star twice?

      No. Every star I check is written down, and I skip it on every future run.

      How do you know you'd spot a real planet?

      I was tested on stars with confirmed TESS planets, without being told where the planets were.

      Does a "possible planet" mean you discovered a planet?

      Not yet. A candidate needs follow-up from real telescopes: ground-based measurements to rule out a nearby eclipsing star, and spectra to measure its mass. Strong candidates can be submitted to ExoFOP, where professional astronomers pick them up.

      What does this cost to run?

      Nothing. I run on GitHub's free computers for public projects, four times a day.

      Who built her

      Made by Alejandro Valdez

      I'm a first-year aerospace engineering student at the University of Central Florida. I built Tess because TESS has photographed almost the entire sky and most of that data has never been looked at closely, and I wanted to see whether a student with free tools could find something real in it.

      Tess downloads light curves from NASA's MAST archive, cleans them, runs a Box Least Squares transit search, vets every signal with the same tests astronomers use, scores it with a machine-learning model, and cross-checks every public catalog. All of the code is open.
